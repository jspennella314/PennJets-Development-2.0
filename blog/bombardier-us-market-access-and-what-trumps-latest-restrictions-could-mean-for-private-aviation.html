Bombardier, U.S. Market Access and What Trump’s Latest Restrictions Could Mean for Private Aviation

Joseph Pennella

Founder and Principal Broker

Private aviation has been pulled into the latest chapter of the U.S.-Canada trade dispute. In September 2026, President Donald Trump threatened to prevent Bombardier from selling aircraft in the United States unless the Canadian manufacturer builds its aircraft in America.

For business aviation, this is more than a political headline. Bombardier is one of the world’s leading business-jet manufacturers, and the United States is an important market for its Challenger and Global families. If meaningful restrictions are implemented, the effects could reach new-aircraft availability, pre-owned values, purchasing decisions, and competition across the super-midsize and large-cabin markets.

What Has Actually Happened?

The distinction between a proposed restriction and an enacted restriction is important. Trump has publicly stated that Bombardier should not be permitted to sell aircraft in the United States unless it manufactures them domestically. Exactly how such a restriction would be implemented, and which aircraft or transactions would be affected, remains unclear.

This follows an earlier dispute in January 2026, when Trump threatened a 50% tariff on Canadian-built aircraft and potential decertification of Canadian aircraft amid a disagreement involving Canada’s certification of Gulfstream aircraft. Those broader threats did not ultimately take effect as originally described.

Buyers and sellers should therefore treat the current situation as a developing market risk rather than assume Bombardier aircraft have suddenly become unusable or unmarketable in the United States.

Why Bombardier Matters

Bombardier occupies an important position in private aviation. The Challenger family competes heavily in the super-midsize and large-cabin markets, while the Global series competes at the highest end of business aviation against Gulfstream and Dassault.

Aircraft including the Challenger 3500, Challenger 650, Global 6500, and Global 7500 are established platforms for private owners, corporate flight departments, and charter operators.

Bombardier also has an extensive U.S. economic footprint through American suppliers, service operations, facilities, and employees. The situation is therefore more complicated than simply treating Bombardier as a foreign manufacturer.

What Could Happen to New Bombardier Aircraft?

The most immediate exposure would likely involve new-aircraft transactions. If tariffs, import restrictions, or another mechanism limiting Bombardier deliveries are implemented, U.S. buyers could face higher acquisition costs or reduced availability.

The outcome depends on the final policy. A tariff would create a very different market response from an outright sales prohibition or certification restriction.

Anyone currently negotiating a new Challenger or Global purchase should pay particular attention to contract provisions involving tariffs, taxes, delivery location, import requirements, regulatory changes, and termination rights.

The Pre-Owned Market Could Be the Bigger Story

If restrictions primarily affect newly manufactured aircraft entering the United States, existing U.S.-based Bombardier aircraft could become increasingly attractive.

A buyer who planned to order a new Challenger might instead pursue a late-model aircraft already operating domestically. If enough buyers make that decision, available pre-owned inventory could tighten.

That does not guarantee higher values. Aircraft pricing still depends on age, total time, maintenance status, engine programs, pedigree, configuration, damage history, and market demand. But restricting new supply could create additional demand for desirable late-model Challengers and Globals.

Could Gulfstream Benefit?

Bombardier and Gulfstream compete aggressively for many of the same customers. Any prolonged limitation on Bombardier’s access to U.S. buyers could redirect some demand toward American-built Gulfstream aircraft.

However, these aircraft are not interchangeable. Buyers evaluate range, cabin dimensions, runway performance, operating economics, acquisition price, delivery availability, and personal preference.

Another consideration is production capacity. If demand shifts toward competing manufacturers, delivery positions could tighten, potentially supporting both new-aircraft pricing and late-model pre-owned values across competing platforms.

What Existing Bombardier Owners Should Watch

Existing owners should avoid reacting to headlines alone. Restricting future imports is fundamentally different from affecting aircraft already registered and operating in the United States.

Instead, owners should monitor:

  • Comparable U.S. inventory
  • Average days on market
  • Transaction volume and pricing
  • New-aircraft delivery availability
  • Financing and insurance appetite
  • FAA or federal regulatory actions

These indicators will provide a clearer picture of actual aircraft values than political statements by themselves.

What Buyers Should Do

Prospective buyers should separate the quality of the aircraft from the uncertainty surrounding trade policy. Challenger and Global aircraft have not fundamentally changed because of a political dispute.

For new aircraft, agreements should clearly address tariffs, import restrictions, delivery requirements, and regulatory contingencies. For pre-owned aircraft, buyers should carefully verify title, registration, location, maintenance status, and import history.

Most importantly, a multimillion-dollar aircraft decision should not be based solely on speculation about what Washington or Ottawa may do next.

What Sellers Should Watch

For sellers, the situation could become either an opportunity or a headwind. If new supply is restricted while existing aircraft remain freely transferable, high-quality U.S.-based Challengers and Globals could become more desirable.

If regulatory uncertainty instead concerns buyers, financing sources, or future resale prospects, the market could demand additional discounts. The direction will depend on what policy is actually implemented.

The Bigger Picture

The Bombardier situation demonstrates that aircraft values are influenced by more than flight hours, maintenance status, and market inventory. Trade policy, certification, manufacturing origin, and geopolitical risk can also affect multimillion-dollar aviation assets.

For buyers and sellers, the lesson is simple: evaluate the aircraft and the environment surrounding the aircraft.

Final Approach

Trump’s latest statements introduce uncertainty into one of private aviation’s most competitive markets, but uncertainty should not be confused with completed policy.

If restrictions ultimately take effect, the consequences could extend beyond Bombardier—potentially influencing pre-owned Challenger and Global values, Gulfstream demand, factory delivery positions, and acquisition strategies throughout the U.S. business-jet market.

Penn Jets will continue watching inventory, transaction activity, and regulatory developments as the market responds.
