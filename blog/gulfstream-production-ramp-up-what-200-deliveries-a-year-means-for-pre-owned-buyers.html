Gulfstream Production Ramp-Up: What 200 Deliveries a Year Means for Pre-Owned Buyers

Joseph Pennella

Founder and Principal Broker

Photo: Wing, CC BY-SA 4.0.

Gulfstream intends to deliver 200 jets in 2030, according to General Dynamics president Danny Deep, speaking on the company’s quarterly investor call (AIN, November 5, 2026). Orders rose 30% in the first nine months of 2026, and analysts put this year’s deliveries at roughly 160 aircraft (Aviation Week, November 2026).

That reads like relief for a market we have described as unusually tight. It is worth being precise about how much relief, and for whom.

The Ramp Is Real, and It Is Not a Flood

Going from about 160 to 200 aircraft is roughly a 25% increase spread over four years. Production has to exceed 200 in 2029 to leave time for exterior paint and cabin completions (AIN, November 5, 2026).

Deep was direct about the constraint: “We have the production and the workforce and the facilities to produce 200 aircraft per year,” he said, adding that suppliers must keep pace. Anything beyond 200 would require new facilities and equipment, which the company would fund if demand warranted (AIN, November 5, 2026). Mark Burns, president of Gulfstream Aerospace, had told NBAA-BACE two weeks earlier that deliveries would reach at least 200 a year in the “not-too-distant future.”

So: more airplanes, four years out, if the supply chain cooperates.

Why It Does Little for the Scarcity We Have Documented

New production and pre-owned scarcity are connected, but loosely, and on a delay.

Nobody shopping a 2015 G550 is cross-shopping a new G800. We reported in October that 21 of 617 G550s were listed, 3.4% of the fleet, and that only two of the 128 airframes built in 2015 or later were available (JETNET, October 16, 2026, and Penn Jets research). A Savannah production line running faster in 2029 does not put a late-model G550 on the market in 2027.

The mechanism that does matter is the trade. Each new delivery to an existing owner can release a used aircraft, and that is how factory output eventually reaches the pre-owned market. But it arrives years later, in the models those buyers are leaving, and the owners we described in our G550 note — 596 of 617 not selling at any price — are not waiting on a delivery slot.

The Supply Story That Actually Bites

The more immediate news in the same report concerns the super-midsize line, and it runs the other way.

The last G280 delivery is expected in mid-2027. The G300 that replaces it is expected to certify later in 2027, with deliveries possibly beginning in late 2027 or early 2028 (AIN, November 5, 2026). Both models are built by Israel Aerospace Industries in Tel Aviv, and Deep said the timing depends on “the situation in the Middle East,” noting that military call-ups have disrupted staffing at that plant.

That is a gap in new super-midsize supply, of uncertain length, arriving in roughly eighteen months.

Super-midsize is where a large share of our buyers actually shop. If new G280 production ends before its replacement is delivering, the pre-owned G280 market tightens, and so does demand for the aircraft buyers consider alongside it — the Challenger 350, the Citation Latitude, the Praetor 500.

Phebe Novakovic, chair and chief executive of General Dynamics, also noted that Middle East customers are showing “some cautious concern” and are “still active in the pipeline but slow to provide orders” (Aviation Week, November 2026). Demand in that region and production in that region are now both exposed to the same situation.

What To Check

  1. Do not reprice a pre-owned aircraft on a 2030 production target. Four years of OEM guidance is not inventory, and plans announced on investor calls change with supply chains and demand.

  2. If you are shopping super-midsize, watch the G280 to G300 gap. A production pause of uncertain length ahead of a 2028 service entry is the kind of thing that moves a pre-owned market before anyone writes about it.

  3. Ask where the airframe is built, not only who sells it. The G280 and G300 are manufactured in Tel Aviv. We have written this autumn about country of manufacture becoming a transaction variable; this is another form of the same exposure.

  4. Think about your exit in the model you are leaving, not the one you are buying. If OEM output rises as planned, the aircraft most affected are the ones currently being traded in. Confirm with your advisor how a longer hold changes your position.

Our View

Two hundred deliveries a year would be a genuine increase in supply, and the order growth behind it suggests demand to absorb it. For the market we cover, it is a 2029 and 2030 story, and a modest one.

The nearer-term news in the same announcement is a hole in super-midsize production, timing dependent on a situation nobody controls. That is the part worth acting on, and it is not in any of the headlines.
